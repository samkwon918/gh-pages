# What Makes Great Companies

A synthesis of the principles that built Apple, Amazon, Nvidia, and BlackRock — distilled into the patterns that separate permanently great businesses from temporarily good ones.

## The Research Base

This page synthesizes patterns from four of the most successful companies ever built:
- [[entities/apple]] — $3T+ valuation, most valuable brand on Earth
- [[entities/amazon]] — from online bookstore to $2T+ everything empire
- [[entities/nvidia]] — from gaming chips to owning the AI era, 95% market share
- [[entities/blackrock]] — from 8 people in 1988 to managing $10T+

These four companies span different industries, different decades, and different models — yet they share the same deep structure.

---

## The 7 Laws of Great Companies

### Law 1: Customer Obsession, Not Competitor Obsession

Every great company on this list is pathologically focused on the end user — not on what rivals are doing.

- Amazon leaves an empty chair in meetings for "the most important person — the customer"
- Apple begins every product with "what does this person deserve to feel?"
- Nvidia built CUDA for developers 15 years before developers knew they needed it
- BlackRock built Aladdin to give clients clarity they didn't know was possible

**The trap:** watching competitors. The race to copy is the race to average. The companies that win are defining a relationship with a customer, not a position relative to a rival.

**Applied to Carity:** who is the customer, exactly? What do they deserve to feel at every touchpoint? What do they not know they need yet?

---

### Law 2: Build the Flywheel, Not Just the Product

None of these companies sells a single thing. They sell systems that reinforce themselves.

- Amazon's flywheel: price → customers → sellers → selection → experience → price
- Apple's ecosystem: iPhone → Mac → AirPods → iCloud → App Store → stickier iPhone
- Nvidia's platform: GPU → CUDA → 100 SDKs → developers → AI demand → GPU
- BlackRock's network: Aladdin → clients → data → better risk models → more clients

The product is the entry point. The flywheel is the business. Most companies never design the flywheel — they just keep selling the entry point harder.

**Applied to Carity:** map the flywheel. What does the first purchase create? How does it generate the second? How does retention lower CAC and fund better product? Where does community fit?

---

### Law 3: Own the Full Stack

Every company on this list controls more of the value chain than seems necessary.

- Apple owns hardware, software, retail, and services
- Amazon owns marketplace, logistics, cloud, and media
- Nvidia owns chips, software stack, and developer tools
- BlackRock owns the investment product AND the risk infrastructure (Aladdin)

Why? Because every layer you cede is a layer that can be used against you, degraded by a partner, or captured by a competitor. Full-stack control is the basis of quality control, pricing power, and moat depth.

**Applied to Carity:** what layers of the customer experience is Carity not controlling that it should? Manufacturing relationship? Fulfillment? Post-purchase communication? Community?

---

### Law 4: Long-Term Thinking Is the Real Strategy

Every great company here made decisions that looked insane in year 1 and indispensable in year 10.

- Amazon ran at near-zero profit for years. AWS was considered a distraction. Prime seemed financially irresponsible.
- Nvidia bet on CUDA in 2006 for a market that barely existed. It paid off in 2023.
- BlackRock bought Barclays Global Investors in 2009 (in a financial crisis) for $13.5B. iShares became the world's largest ETF business.
- Apple killed the iPod with the iPhone. Jobs destroyed his own best product before a competitor could.

The pattern: the decisions that define greatness feel costly and uncertain when made, and obvious in hindsight. The companies that make them do so because they're optimizing for a future state, not the current quarter.

**Applied to Carity:** what is the decision Carity could make today that would feel uncomfortable in year 1 and essential in year 5? What's the CUDA bet?

---

### Law 5: Build a Moat Before You Need One

Moats take years. You start building when you're small, when it seems unnecessary, when the market isn't attacking you yet. By the time you need the moat, it's too late to start building.

- Nvidia's CUDA moat took 17 years to become obvious. But it was built from year 1.
- Apple's ecosystem moat was seeded with the iPod + iTunes combo in 2001. The iPhone just activated it.
- BlackRock's Aladdin moat was built quietly for 20 years before anyone realized the industry ran on it.
- Amazon's Prime moat was considered a loss leader. It became the most effective customer retention mechanism in e-commerce history.

**Applied to Carity:** the moat decisions are made now, not when Carity is 10× bigger. Brand identity, customer data, community, proprietary experience — these compound. Start now.

---

### Law 6: Self-Disruption Is Survival

Every one of these companies disrupted itself before being disrupted.

- Apple killed the iPod with the iPhone (which also threatened the Mac)
- Amazon created AWS, which now competes with its own retail infrastructure
- Nvidia cannibalizes its GPU generations every 12–18 months
- BlackRock acquired ETF businesses that challenged its own active management products

The philosophy: *if someone is going to make you obsolete, make yourself obsolete first.* This requires a level of ego-less clarity about the business that most founders and executives don't have.

**Applied to Carity:** what would a competitor build to take Carity's customers? Build that first.

---

### Law 7: Never Stop Learning — The World Is Always Changing

- Fink studies 90 minutes every morning. Runs $10T.
- Bezos said Day 1 applies always — the moment you think you've figured it out, you've started dying.
- Huang treats every Nvidia year like the company could be disrupted in 30 days.
- Jobs studied calligraphy, Zen Buddhism, and typography and brought them into Apple products.

The world is changing faster than any static body of knowledge can accommodate. The leaders of great companies treat learning not as a phase but as the permanent operating condition.

**Applied to Carity:** this wiki is part of that practice. Every session, every source ingested, every question asked compounds the knowledge base. The discipline of using this wiki is itself a competitive advantage.

---

## The One Sentence

> Great companies are built by people who are obsessed with the customer, think in decades, design compounding systems, build moats while they don't need them, and never stop learning or adapting.
