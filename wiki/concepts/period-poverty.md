# Period Poverty

The inability to afford menstrual hygiene products — a worsening crisis affecting tens of millions of women and girls in the US, and the core market problem that Carity exists to solve.

## The Scale of the Problem

- **42%** of US women have experienced period poverty in their lifetime (2025) — up from 35% in 2023
- **1 in 4** students currently struggle to afford period products
- **1 in 3 teens** lack access to menstruation products (American Academy of Pediatrics)
- **11.9 million** females affected in the US, including **25% of all adolescents**
- **39%** of teens report they can't perform their best schoolwork due to lack of access

The trend is worsening. More women are struggling to afford period products in 2025 than in 2023. Inflation, cost-of-living increases, and rising product prices are compounding the problem.

## Who It Affects Most

Period poverty does not affect women equally:

| Group | Affected rate |
|---|---|
| White women | ~8% struggle to afford period products |
| Black women | ~23% |
| Latina women | ~24% |
| Teens (all) | 1 in 3 lack access |

Black and Latina women are affected at 3× the rate of white women. This is both a health equity issue and a market reality: the most underserved customers are those Carity is specifically built to reach.

## The Health and Education Consequences

Lack of access to period products causes:
- School absences (girls miss school because they cannot manage their period safely)
- Lost work days (same effect for working women)
- Urinary tract infections and bacterial vaginosis (using products longer than safe, or improvising)
- Psychological impact: 59% of teens feel personally affected by the stigma of menstruation

The harm is not abstract. It is educational, economic, and medical.

## Why Organic Matters Even More for This Population

The conventional wisdom is: organic period care is a premium for the wealthy. This is backwards. The women who can least afford synthetic chemical exposure to sensitive tissue are those with the least access to healthcare when something goes wrong. Organic cotton pads reduce irritation, chemical exposure, and associated health risks — making them *more* important, not less, for women with limited healthcare access.

Carity is correcting a market failure: organic quality has been priced for affluence when it's most needed by those who can't afford the alternatives' health consequences.

## The Institutional Opportunity

Organizations serving women in period poverty:
- **Schools / Title I programs** — millions of low-income students need product access
- **Women's shelters** — period poverty is near-universal in shelter populations
- **Food banks** — period products are among the most-requested non-food items
- **Community health organizations** — actively sourcing bulk affordable period care
- **WIC programs, SNAP-adjacent orgs** — serving exactly Carity's demographic

These organizations are an underutilized channel. A single contract with a school district or shelter network can move hundreds of units and create a brand halo that word-of-mouth advertising cannot buy.

## The Market Narrative Carity Can Own

> "Every woman deserves organic period care. We made it affordable."

This is a narrative no competitor has claimed. Rael, Honest, Cora, L. — they all speak to the top of the market. They use wellness influencers, clean aesthetics, and premium pricing signals. None of them have said "this is for *every* woman."

Carity can own that statement. And once it owns it, the brand becomes a movement — not just a product.
